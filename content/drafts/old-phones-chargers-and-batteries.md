It might be a kitchen drawer, or a shoebox on a closet shelf. Two retired phones, a tangle of chargers for devices nobody owns anymore, a pair of earbuds with one bud missing, and a few AA batteries of unknown charge. Nobody decided to keep any of it. It just never left.

Clearing it out takes a roll of tape, a few minutes per phone, and one trip to a drop-off.

## How big the pile is

The UN's [Global E-waste Monitor 2024](https://ewastemonitor.info/the-global-e-waste-monitor-2024/), published by ITU and UNITAR, defines e-waste as any discarded product with a plug or a battery. By that definition the world produced 62 million tonnes of it in 2022. Less than a quarter, 22.3%, was documented as properly collected and recycled, and the report expects that share to fall to 20% by 2030, because the pile is growing faster than the recycling.

## What's inside a phone

A phone is a very small mine. The EPA, [citing a 2006 US Geological Survey estimate](https://www.epa.gov/recycle/electronics-donation-and-recycling), says that recycling one million cell phones recovers 35,000 pounds of copper, 772 pounds of silver, 75 pounds of gold and 33 pounds of palladium. Divide the gold by a million and it comes to about 34 milligrams a phone, which is why nobody gets rich from their own drawer. The value appears only at volume.

The battery is the part worth worrying about. The EPA [describes the cobalt, graphite and lithium in lithium-ion batteries](https://www.epa.gov/recycle/used-lithium-ion-batteries) as critical minerals, and says that when these batteries are thrown in the trash, "we lose these critical resources outright."

## Keep the one in your pocket a while longer

The lowest-waste phone is usually the one you already own. Apple's [Product Environmental Report for the iPhone 17](https://www.apple.com/environment/pdf/products/iphone/iPhone_17_PER_Sept2025.pdf) puts the 256GB model at 55 kg of CO2e over its life, and that figure assumes three years of use. Three years is Apple's assumption. A phone kept for five gets more use out of the same manufacturing.

The EPA gives the same advice for computers: [consider upgrading the hardware or software](https://www.epa.gov/recycle/electronics-donation-and-recycling) before buying a new one. If you want to see where a phone sits next to everything else your household uses, the [Green Home guides](/guides-and-tips/green-home) include a household carbon footprint calculator.

## Erase it before it goes

An old phone is also a copy of your life you've stopped looking after. Wipe it before it leaves the house.

On an iPhone, Apple's [checklist](https://support.apple.com/en-us/109511) is to back it up, unpair any Apple Watch, sign out of your Apple Account, and then go to Settings, General, Transfer or Reset iPhone, and tap Erase All Content and Settings.

On Android, Google's [factory reset guide](https://support.google.com/android/answer/6088915?hl=en) warns that a reset erases all of your data from the phone. It asks you to make sure you know the Google Account and password on the phone first, and to back up anything you want to keep to that account. Charge the phone to at least 70% before you start; Google says the reset can take up to an hour.

The two certification standards the EPA lists for electronics recyclers, R2 and e-Stewards, both [require the destruction of all data](https://www.epa.gov/electronics-batteries-management/certified-electronics-recyclers) on the devices a certified recycler handles. That is worth knowing if one of the phones in the drawer no longer turns on.

## Not the trash, and not the recycling bin either

Lithium-ion batteries, and devices with them inside, [should not go in household garbage or recycling bins](https://www.epa.gov/recycle/used-lithium-ion-batteries), according to the EPA. In a truck or on a sorting line the battery can be crushed or damaged, and a damaged battery is a fire hazard. That holds for a battery that seems flat, too: "even used batteries can have enough energy to injure or start fires."

The alternative is cheap. Put each battery, or each device with a battery in it, in its own plastic bag, or cover the terminals with non-conductive tape such as electrical tape. Then take them to a drop-off. [Call2Recycle and Earth911](https://www.epa.gov/recycle/used-lithium-ion-batteries) can find one nearby. Retailer take-back programs and your local household hazardous waste collection are the other routes the agency names.

## Sorting the rest of the drawer

Lithium is the thing to look for. The type is identified by the battery's marking rather than its shape, so the advice on the EPA's household battery page is to [check the battery for the word "lithium"](https://www.epa.gov/recycle/used-household-batteries). Most coin and button cells, the kind in watches and car key fobs, are now made with lithium too. They are also a swallowing hazard for young children, so keep them out of reach while they wait for the drop-off.

Plain alkaline AAs and AAAs are the exception. On the same page, the EPA says that in most communities they can safely go in household trash, though its recommendation is still to send them to a battery recycler or ask your local solid waste authority.

The chargers, cables and the earbuds with one bud left all have a plug or a battery, which makes them e-waste by the UN's definition. They go with the phones. Of the collection finders the EPA lists, [GreenerGadgets accepts all types of electronics](https://www.epa.gov/electronics-batteries-management/certified-electronics-recyclers), and Call2Recycle takes cell phones as well as batteries.

## What stays behind

After one trip, the drawer holds a roll of electrical tape and one charging cable that still fits something in the house. Probably. Nobody is quite sure what.
