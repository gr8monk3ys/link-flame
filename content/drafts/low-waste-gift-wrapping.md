# Gift wrapping can be reused instead of thrown away

Gift wrap has one of the shortest lives of anything in the house. It gets bought, folded around a present, admired for a few seconds and torn off. Between the holidays and every birthday in the year, that adds up to a lot of paper and ribbon that exists only to be thrown out.

Low-waste wrapping doesn't mean giving up on presents that look special. It means choosing wrapping that gets used again, or that your local program will actually take once it's done.

## Start with what you already have

### Save and reuse paper, bags and boxes
The lowest-waste wrapping is wrapping that already exists. Smooth out paper from presents you receive and fold it flat, and keep gift bags and sturdy boxes in good condition. A drawer set aside for "wrapping to reuse" turns this from a good intention into a habit.

### Use paper from around the house
Brown kraft paper from packaging, old maps and the comics pages all make distinctive wrapping, and so do children's drawings. Brown paper in particular looks deliberate with a simple tie and a sprig of greenery.

### Make the container part of the gift
A jar, a mixing bowl or a tote bag can hold the present and stay useful long after it's opened. Our [Measured, Not Promised Tote](/products) is an organic cotton tote that works this way: fill it with a food gift or a few small items and the bag is part of the present. Containers suit food gifts best, and anything made up of several small pieces.

## Wrap with fabric

### Try furoshiki-style wrapping
Furoshiki is a traditional Japanese technique for wrapping and carrying things in a square of cloth. A few simple knots hold the fabric in place without any tape, and the cloth can be untied and used again for the next present.

You don't need special fabric to start. A scarf or a pillowcase works, and so does a dish towel. Square pieces are easiest: place the gift in the middle, bring two opposite corners together and tie them, then repeat with the other two corners.

### Wrap gifts in something useful
A wrap that is also part of the present doubles its value. For someone who cooks, a gift wrapped in a dish towel and tied with kitchen twine is two presents in one.

## Ties, tape and tags

### Skip the plastic ribbon
Curling ribbon and synthetic bows rarely survive a second use. Cotton string and fabric ribbon can be saved and tied again for years, and natural fibers can go in a home compost pile once they're worn out.

### Use less tape, or none
Tape often tears the paper when it comes off, which makes reuse harder. Folding paper neatly and tying it with string can hold it closed without tape at all. If you do need tape, a few short pieces come off far more cleanly than long strips, and paper-based tape is another option.

### Make tags from old cards
Last year's greeting cards can be cut into gift tags: trim the picture side to shape, punch a hole and thread through some string. Writing straight onto the wrapping paper works too.

## Check what your program accepts

Not all gift wrap is paper all the way through. Wrap with foil, glitter or a shiny coating is a different material from plain paper, and curbside programs in the US differ on what they take. Look up your city or county's recycling guide before anything goes in the bin, and pull off tape and bows first.

One rule of thumb people use is the scrunch test: scrunch the paper into a ball, and if it springs back open it probably has a plastic coating. Treat it as a hint, not a substitute for your program's own list.

Plain, uncoated paper that can't be reused can also go in a home compost pile, torn into small pieces. Our [Ultimate Guide to Composting](/blogs/ultimate-guide-to-composting) covers what to add and how to keep a pile balanced.

## Give gifts that need less wrapping

Some gifts need no wrapping at all. An experience or a homemade meal skips the paper entirely, and so does a [gift card](/gift-cards) sent by email. When you do give a physical gift, choosing something durable and genuinely wanted keeps it out of the trash, which matters more than the paper around it.

For gift ideas that help someone else cut waste, the [zero-waste bathroom swaps](/blogs/zero-waste-bathroom-swaps) post is a good place to start, and the [Zero Waste guides](/guides-and-tips/zero-waste) go further on reducing waste around the home.

The paper you saved from last year is still in the drawer, folded flat, waiting for this year's first present.
